## Turn 1 — Ruby (Aurelie)

Aurelie made sure to avoid Eris as much as she could for the next three days. That turned out to be easier than she expected, because starting in the morning, the entire household started preparations for their travel. They'd be riding horses — Prince was to only travel at nights, catching up to them while they slept and waiting during the day. Which, conveniently for Eris (if anyone asked Aurelie, not that anyone did) meant that she'd be close to the dragon only in the mornings before they moved on. She said nothing about it, and listened quietly, when Eris brought maps to her room and ordered that she study the path they were going to take. He expected her to know where to hide, and which stretches of the duct were safe from most patrols and be able to join him back at one of the marked rendezvous points, should they get separated. If anyone asked Aurelie, not that anyone did, she'd say he was being paranoid.   
There were provisions packed, and dresses measured and folded neatly in a coffer, because as Briar informed 'Master said he won't be seeing you in anything Summer gives you, Miss'. And then, the evening before they were to leave, there was the issue of the carriage. Eris announced over dinner, that while the dragon was free to travel on his own for most of the way, once they got close to Adriata, he would be required to hide in a carriage that was officially — and unofficially as well, though to lesser extent — carrying their luggage. He also informed, with clipped reluctance, that Caelum would be driving the carriage, because Aurelie's combat skills still left a lot to be desired, so the weaponsmaster would be required to travel with them until such time that he was no longer needed. Aurelie perked up at the mention of Caelum, but Prince announced he categorically refuses to get into 'that wooden contraption' and that he'd rather swallow his own tail. The dinner ended tense, and neither the High Fae nor the dragon conceded, which meant that the issue would be decided later along the way.

The day of departure started damp and grey, and Aurelie felt wet and gloomy even before she stepped foot outside. Tomas was already waiting, holding Brille's reins. That had also been a discussion between her and Eris — he wanted a more docile horse of her, she announced that she wouldn't get on the back of any horse other than Brille — only on this he finally conceded as if she was giving him enough of a headache that he decided this one thing wasn't worth fighting over. She took Brille's reins from Tomas, and the old fae hugged her in a warm, slightly awkward way. 
"Be well, Miss. Take care of Brille, and she'll take care of you. And come back to us in Ashgrove." Then he stepped back, and when Aurelie glanced behind him, she realized that Briar and a few others gathered on the steps as well. She smiled, and waved to them. With a small spark of surprise she realized, that there was a part of her that didn't want to leave those people, this place, however horrible its master was.
"You too Tomas. Give the horses apples from me, okay?"
Then she mounted the mare. The horse shifted its weight in place a few times, nickering and testing the give in the reins, but Aurelie sat deep in the saddle and let Brille make a small circle, returning her back to the same place. 
A shadow fell across the yard, as Prince stretched his wings on the balcony and swept down to stop close enough to the mare, that Brille reared up and Aurelie felt a flicker of panic for a second, before she got her under control.
{Do you enjoy this?} She sent to Prince irritably. The faebane bracelets were removed for the road, out of necessity. They needed to be able to communicate over long distances.
{Immensely.} The dragon replied, dry and unrepentant, narrowing his cat-like eyes on the horse. {You shouldn't be riding on this.}
{Oh, what should I be riding on then? The carriage?}
{You should—} He cut off, but Aurelie had already seen the thought forming in his mind. Her, on his back, right where the neck met his shoulders, high up in the air. Her stomach dropped.
{No, absolutely not. I have no intention of killing myself.}
The dragon shifted his gaze from the horse to her, and she felt a spark of sorrow and regret through the bond.
{I would have never let you get hurt.}
And then he stalked to the side, ready to watch her leave and wait for the entire day until he was allowed to follow.

## Turn 2 — Vesper (Departure from Ashgrove, the Mile-Stone & Caelum)

The heavy oak doors of the lodge didn't slam so much as click shut with the quiet, oiled finality of a rifle bolt.

Eris stood on the top stone step for a beat, pulling on a pair of dark leather riding gloves, his amber eyes taking in the entire courtyard at once—Tomas stepping back with suspiciously damp eyes, Briar lingering by the pillar with her apron twisted in her fingers, the auburn mare still dancing sideways from the downdraft of feathered wings, and the white-and-ice-blue beast himself stalking to the side of the yard like an oversized, golden-horned cat that had been denied the cream.

He wore no court gold for the road. Dark, weather-treated wool the color of dried blood, high black boots, a long travelling cloak clasped at the throat with plain blackened iron, and a bastard sword strapped across his saddle rather than his hip. His copper-red hair was tied back low at the nape of his neck to keep the damp mist out of his eyes.

"If you're quite finished terrifying the livestock," Eris drawled down the steps, not bothering to look directly at the dragon as he descended onto the wet gravel, "try to remember that the wards end at the mile-stone. If I feel so much as a ripple of unshielded dragon-magic before moonrise, you'll be riding into Adriata in that carriage whether we have to grease your scales or fold your wings like a wet umbrella."

Across the yard, beside the heavy four-horse baggage coach waiting at the mouth of the drive, Caelum didn't bother hiding the faint twitch at the corner of his mouth. The fox-touched weaponsmaster was already settled on the high driver's box in worn brown leather and an oiled cloak, his russet-furred fox ears angled forward through his scruffy hair as he looped the long coaching reins through his scarred fingers. When he caught Aurelie's eye across the mist, one russet ear tipped slightly and he gave her a quiet, grounding nod—clearly glad to be trading the suffocating air of Ashgrove's dining room for four days of open timber, even if Eris had put him on the coach box specifically to keep fifteen feet of luggage and four horses between the instructor and his student.

Eris didn't spare Caelum a glance. He walked straight to his own mount—the massive, coal-black Autumn gelding that didn't so much as flick an ear at the white-and-blue dragon twenty paces away—and swung into the saddle in one fluid, unhurried motion.

Instead of turning down the drive immediately, he walked the black gelding alongside Brille, close enough that the iron stirrup of his boot brushed the toe of Aurelie's riding boot. The mare pinned her ears at the larger horse, and Eris's mouth curved into the faintest, sharpest edge of a smirk as he looked down at Aurelie's hands on the leather.

"Good seat," he remarked quietly, pitched low enough that neither Tomas nor Caelum could hear over the jingle of harness brass. "Better than three weeks ago. Though if she throws you into a ravine because your companion decided to show off his wingspan, don't expect me to climb down after you."

His amber gaze flicked up from her hands to her face—taking in the damp mist clinging to her lashes, the bare wrists under her cuffs where the dull grey faebane had sat for a month, and the quiet, stubborn set of her jaw that had kept her out of his way for three straight days. *Three days of polite, ghost-quiet obedience,* he thought with a curl of dark irritation under his ribs, *and she still looks at me as if she's calculating the exact weight of the rock she'd drop on my head if I slipped on the mountain pass.*

"Stay two lengths behind me until we clear the timberline, Lilly. Brennec has the rear." He leaned a fraction closer in the saddle, the warmth of roasted chestnut and woodsmoke cutting straight through the cold morning drizzle. "And keep your voice out of the air once we cross the ward-line. Mental bonds leave a scent on the wind if you shout down them. Short thoughts. Quiet ones. Unless you'd like every border hound between here and the southern pass to know what you're carrying."

Without waiting for her agreement, he touched his heel to the black gelding's flank and turned them down the narrow, mist-choked forest road—leaving Ashgrove, Tomas, and the white-and-ice-blue dragon behind the closing copper canopy.

***

For the first twenty minutes, the woods were silent save for the steady, muffled thud of hooves on wet pine needles and the distant creak of Caelum's carriage wheels winding down the switchbacks behind them.

Then they crossed the mile-stone.

There was no gate, no painted border-post—only an ancient, moss-choked standing stone half-buried in the ferns at the edge of the creek. The moment Brille's front hooves splashed across the water, the air changed. The warm, bell-like hush that had wrapped Ashgrove for three weeks vanished between one breath and the next. Out here, the Autumn forest felt raw, wild, and startlingly loud: the wind hissed sharper through the high branches, the damp cold bit deeper through wool, and at the very back of her skull, the long, stretching tether connecting her to the courtyard a mile behind them suddenly hummed against the open, unwarded sky.

Ahead of her, Eris didn't look back, though one gloved hand rested idly on his thigh as the road began to climb toward the southern ridge.

Fifteen yards behind her, with the bend in the pines finally blocking the High Lord's heir from view, Caelum eased the carriage team down to a walk. His russet fox ears swiveled once toward the empty sky behind them before he spoke in that low, slightly rough voice that carried just far enough to reach Brille's saddle:

"Shoulders down, Lilly. You've been bracing your right arm since we passed the creek." A pause, dry and unhurried as the creak of the leather harness. "He'll catch up after dark. And if he decides to eat one of the carriage wheels when we reach the coast, I'll look the other way."

## Turn 4 — Vesper (Caelum on the Coach Box)

At her smile, the corner of Caelum's mouth creased into something genuine—not the polite, blank mask the household wore when the High Lord's heir was in earshot, but the dry, weathered warmth she'd seen across the training yard whenever she finally landed a clean parry.

He shifted all four leather ribbons into his left hand with the easy habit of a man who had driven supply wagons through border ambushes long before he was ever named Master-at-Arms, and rested his right forearm across his knee, leaning down slightly from the high box so he didn't have to raise his voice above the crunch of wet gravel and the jingle of the trace chains.

"Ashgrove was built to make people forget what's outside the fence," Caelum said quietly, his amber-brown eyes flicking over Brille's ears to make sure the mare wasn't crowding the near-side wheel horse. "Warm bread, dry beds, wards thick enough to choke a hound's nose. Doesn't mean your instincts went soft, Lilly. Just means you finally got some sleep."

When she glanced back toward the unseen estate—toward the mile of raw, unwarded pine and mist now stretching between her and the courtyard where Prince waited—Caelum's russet fox ears swiveled back in the same direction for a second. Even without a bond of his own, a hunter who had spent a century and a half reading the woods knew what a tethered creature looked like when the line pulled taut.

Then she spoke about the carriage.

*To him, it's like asking you to give up all your weapons.*

Caelum went still for a beat on the driver's bench. The humour didn't leave his face, exactly, but it settled into something older and much more serious. His fingers flexed once against the oiled leather of the reins—right above the worn ash-wood stave of his unstrung longbow strapped beside the footboard, and the twin bone-hilted hunting knives at his belt that he didn't even take off to eat.

"Aye," Caelum murmured, his rough voice dropping low enough that the sound belonged entirely to the space between Brille's saddle and the coach wheel. "I figured as much at dinner."

One russet ear twitched sideways, angling toward the bend in the road forty paces ahead where the dark silhouette of Eris's coal-black gelding was just visible through the drifting red mist, climbing the grade without slowing.

"Give up my blades and get locked in a wooden box while someone else holds the key?" Caelum let out a quiet, gravelly breath that was half-laugh, half-snort. "I'd put a boot through the panelling before the horses cleared the gate. And your friend has considerably sharper boots than I do."

He looked back down at her, his scarred brow furrowing a fraction as he weighed how much to say with the wind blowing uphill toward High Fae ears.

"Thing is, Lilly... Eris knows that too."

Caelum tilted his chin toward the heavy coach behind him—piled with cedar dress-coffers, spare tack, and canvas-lashed crates, with the hollowed central berth beneath the tarp that the stablehands had spent yesterday afternoon reinforcing with iron brackets.

"I've ridden behind him for a hundred and forty years," Caelum said, his tone matter-of-fact, stripped of either flattery or spite. "When he genuinely expects a soldier—or a beast—to obey an order at the point of a spear, he doesn't bring it up over roast venison three days before the border. He picked that fight at the table because he wanted to see where the dragon's teeth were before we hit Summer land."

His russet ears angled forward again as the road narrowed beside atumbling mountain run, the cold spray misting over Brille's fetlocks.

"It's not Eris's pride your friend has to worry about at the coast," Caelum added quietly. "It's Tarquin's shore-sentries. Summer's water-scryers can read a shadow on the surf from three leagues out, and since that bounty paper went up in the autumn markets, half the border captains between here and Adriata are looking for a girl and a winged scaled thing. If Prince flies over the white cliffs in daylight—or even under a clear moon—every harpoon-tower on the bay will loose before anyone asks whose seal we're carrying."

He tapped his boot heel lightly against the iron brake-bar beside the box, where a rolled bundle of heavy, salt-bleached canvas—stamped with the blue wave-crest of an Adriata merchant house rather than Autumn's fox-and-flame—was lashed beneath the seat.

"Nobody's locking him in the dark, Lilly. Whatever Eris barked across the soup tureen." A faint, sideways glint returned to the weaponsmaster's amber-brown eyes. "When we reach the coastal duct on the fourth day, you're going to tell that overprotective lizard of yours that he's not surrendering his weapons—he's taking point inside a siege-engine while we smuggle him past the stupidest guards in Prythian. Frame it like a hunt instead of a cage. Predators understand cover."

Up ahead, where the road crested a shelf of wet black slate beneath an arch of blazing copper maples, the coal-black gelding stopped and turned broadside across the trail.

Eris didn't ride back down to them, nor did he raise his voice. He simply sat his saddle in the drifting mist—dark crimson wool, blackened iron, one gloved hand resting on his pommel—and let his sharp amber gaze drop down the slope, taking in Aurelie riding abreast of the lead harness horse and Caelum leaning over the footboard.

For three slow seconds, Eris's eyes held hers through the falling red leaves. He didn't have to say *Two lengths, Aurelie* out loud; the dry, unimpressed arch of one copper brow said it across forty yards of mountain air.

Beside her, Caelum's fox ears flattened a fraction in shameless amusement as he clicked his tongue to the four-horse team.

"Eyes front, student," Caelum murmured out of the side of his mouth, straightening on the box as if they'd spent the entire climb discussing nothing more treasonous than rein-tension. "Before he decides I'm teaching you bad habits and makes *me* ride in the luggage berth."

## Turn 5 — Ruby (Aurelie)

Aurelie sighed slightly, but she also straightened in her saddle and touched her heels to Brille's sides, urging the mare forward, placing her ahead of the wagon but still a horse-length behind Eris. She could feel Prince, and for a moment wanted to reach out to him on instinct, but then remembered what Eris said about the bond dragging downwind and decided that it wasn't worth the risk. Instead, she thought about Caelum's words, and how to try to convince the dragon to comply. What she found funny was that — since the first moment Eris brought her to Ashgrove — everyone assumed that she owned the dragon. That he was like her familiar, or a pet, akin to what Eris's hounds were to him. Meanwhile Aurelie often felt like she belonged to Prince, more than he to her. And at the very least, she had just as much ability to stop him when he decided something as everyone else. 
They climbed higher up, and at some point, Aurelie spotted a collection of smokes puffing out from the chimneys of what looked like a small village, hugged into the side of one of the hills. She wondered what kind of beings lived there, and wished — suddenly, selfishly — that she could just ride over there and stay there for a few days, learning their songs and their way of life. She was excited to see the Summer Court, but she highly doubted it could be as beautiful as Autumn, even on a day as dreary as this one, with a drizzle falling from the clouds since dawn. 
She wasn't sure how long they'd ridden, but it had to be a few hours at least, when a thought came to her. She clicked at Brille, forcing her to come nose to nose with Eris's gelding.
"At Summer, I want you to introduce me as Aurelie Delacroix." She said to him, without any preamble. "They will see my dragon, and the moment they do, neither him nor me will be a secret anymore. I don't want to hide under a fake name from people who we want to be our— your allies."

## Turn 6 — Vesper (Eris & "Lilly" on the Docks)

For three hours, the only proof Eris had paid any attention to her at all was the pace of his horse.

Whenever the grade steepened through the wet copper beech and pine, the big black gelding shortened its stride just enough that Brille never had to scramble over the slick limestone roots to keep her two-length gap. When Aurelie's gaze had drifted down into the hollow where the crofters' chimneys were sending up thin, blue ribbons of birch-smoke against the hillside, Eris hadn't turned his head—not by an inch—yet two minutes later, his voice had floated back over the drizzle as if he had eyes in the back of his skull.

"Wood-wrights and cider-makers," he had remarked to the empty air between his gelding's ears. "Lesser fae who pay their tithe in seasoned ash-wood and barrel staves so my father's tax-collectors have no excuse to ride up the mud-track. Half of them haven't seen a High Fae in forty years. If you rode down there for three days, they'd spend the first two barring their shutters and the third trying to feed you enough honey-cake to keep your dragon from eating their goats."

He hadn't said another word after that. He had let the silence stretch out again, thick with the smell of wet wool, crushed bracken, and horse-steam—until the sudden click of her tongue and the quick, splashing surge of hooves brought the auburn mare right up beside his stirrup.

The black gelding pinned one ear at Brille's intrusion. Eris's hand tightened a fraction on the wet leather reins, checking the warhorse before it could snap at the mare's nose.

He turned his head slowly.

For a beat, he didn't speak. He just looked at her—at the rain-darkened tendrils of chestnut hair plastered to her cheekbones, the flush the cold mountain climb had put high on her skin, and the way her chin was tilted up at him as if *she* were the one leading an armed escort across a hostile border.

*Three days,* Eris thought, a sudden, sharp heat prickling beneath his breastbone that had nothing to do with the climb. *Three days of slipping around Ashgrove's corridors like a mouse in the wainscoting so she wouldn't have to look me in the eye after the dining room—and the first time she opens her mouth on the road, it's to issue me marching orders.*

Any one of his brothers would have been knocked out of his saddle for using that tone with him. Half of his father's commanders wouldn't have dared ride up beside his stirrup uninvited. And yet here she sat on a half-trained mare in the pouring mist, fifty miles from everything she knew, dictating terms to the heir of the Autumn Court with rain dripping off the tip of her nose.

Worse—and infinitely more irritating—was the way his blood answered it. A slow, predatory pull low in his gut, unwanted and entirely unbidden, at the sheer, reckless brass of her. And underneath that, sharper still, the catch of a single syllable she had tried to swallow back:

*Our— your allies.*

Eris's amber eyes narrowed slightly. He let his gelding match Brille's walk stride for stride along the narrow ledge of the mountain road, close enough that the heavy wet wool of his travelling cloak brushed against her knee with every roll of the horses' shoulders.

"You speak to me for the first time since Tuesday," Eris said, his voice a low, silk-over-flint murmur pitched so Caelum, twenty yards back around the bend of the wagon-track, couldn't catch a word of it, "and you don't even bother with a *good morning*. Tell me, *Lady Delacroix*—did you spend those three quiet days in your room studying my maps, or just practicing that speech in the mirror?"

He didn't give her time to bristle before he guided the black horse half a foot closer, crowding Brille just enough toward the rock wall on the inside of the pass that Aurelie had to look straight into his face instead of the road ahead.

"Do you know what Adriata is, Aurelie?" The use of her real name on his tongue was deliberate—a slow, dark test of its weight in the wet mountain air. "It isn't Ashgrove. It isn't a hidden hunting lodge behind three layers of blood-wards and forty miles of timber. It is the largest deep-water port in the southern courts. Every week, merchant hulls from the mortal continent drop anchor in Tarquin's harbor to trade grain and southern wine for Fae timber and pearls."

His gaze dropped for a fraction of a second to her mouth, then snapped back to her eyes, cold and uncompromising.

"Which means Queen Ismena's gold spends just as well on the docks of Adriata as it does across the Wall. Right now, the bounty hunters and my father's informers know only that a nameless mortal girl with a white dragon vanished into the eastern marches. The moment I walk into Tarquin's sea-palace and announce *Aurelie Delacroix* to a hall full of courtiers and harbor-servants, your aunt's ten-thousand-crown price tag has a map, an address, and a harbor master willing to sell your berth before the tide turns."

He paused, the leather of his glove creaking faintly as he shifted the reins. Behind them, the heavy iron rims of Caelum's coach ground over a patch of shale, keeping a steady, tactful distance.

And yet—despite every piece of cold, ruthless court calculation he had just laid out—his jaw tightened. Because she wasn't wrong about one thing.

Tarquin was not Beron. The young High Lord of Summer had an infuriating, idealistic streak a mile wide, and if he caught the scent of a lie in his own throne room while a golden-horned dragon was circling his marble spires, the alliance would be dead before the wine was poured.

*And she said 'our,'* Eris thought again, irritably aware of the warmth of her leg through two layers of damp wool where the horses swayed together. *She corrected herself as if it burned her tongue, the stubborn little wildcat, because she still wants to pretend she's only here at the point of a sword.*

"Tarquin doesn't need to be fed a nursery name in private," Eris said, his voice dropping half an octave as the road crested a stony rise and the wind off the southern ridges hit their faces—still cold, still smelling of pine, but carrying the faintest, distant ghost of warmer air beyond the peaks. "When the doors to his council chamber are shut and his sea-wards are up, you may give him every syllable of your name, your bloodline, and whatever grievances you please. You can introduce your dragon to him yourself, assuming the beast hasn't eaten one of his harbor-ships by then."

He leaned a fraction across the gap between their saddles, his amber eyes locking onto hers with sudden, unblinking intensity.

"But on the docks, in the streets, and in front of every servant pouring wine in the outer halls, you are *Lilly*. Not because I am hiding you from *our* allies—" he let that word land between them like a coin dropped on stone, the corner of his mouth curving into a dangerous, knowing half-smile at the way he echoed her slip, "—but because I didn't drag you across four days of mountain mud just to spend my evenings fishing Queen Ismena's hired knives out of your ribs."

He straightened in the saddle as the trail widened slightly, though he didn't pull his horse away from hers.

"Does that satisfy your sudden passion for diplomacy, or were you planning to nail your family crest to the carriage door as well?"

## Turn 7 — Ruby (Aurelie)

Aurelie did begin to bristle, but Eris cut it before it could take any definitive shape. So she listened and shortened the reigns, because she could feel Brille getting uneasy between her legs at the sudden lack of space on both her sides. She focused on not letting the mare take it too personally and even placed her right hand, hidden under a black leather glove, on the horses's neck and patted it gently. 
Her brow furrowed slightly, when Eris laid out his rules, and she couldn't help but wonder what kind of person High Lord of Summer was, considering how condescending Eris's tone was when he talked about him.
And then the ember-haired male said 'just to spend my evenings fishing Queen Ismena's hired knives out of your ribs' and Aurelie's brow arched up.
"First, I do hope that Lord Tarquin is capable of protecting his guests from some human assassins. Otherwise, he'd be a pretty poor excuse of a High Lord." She said, tipping her chin up and looking between Brille's ears instead of at Eris.
"Second," she went quiet for so long that for a moment it didn't seem that she'd finish. "I don't think my family wants anything to do with me. They want the dragon. Just like you, Queen Ismena and everyone else. So no. I want nothing to do with that crest."
And then she kicked Brille's flank strong enough that the mare nickered irritably and went from a steady trot into long canter for a few paces, pushing forward and getting at least a few yards between them. The road was winding, but it wasn't exactly a merchant duct, there weren't any forks and Aurelie was pretty sure she was able to follow a straight road.

## Turn 8 — Vesper (The Pack-Bridge)

For three heartbeats after Brille's iron shoes kicked up a spray of wet gravel and pine needles across his gelding's knees, Eris didn't move a muscle.

The black warhorse tossed its head, snorting hotly at the insult of being passed by a smaller mare, muscles bunching under Eris's thighs to surge after her. Eris held the gelding back with two fingers on the rein.

His amber eyes stayed locked on the center of Aurelie's dark green cloak as she cantered fifteen, twenty, thirty yards ahead of him down the mist-hung track.

*They want the dragon. Just like you, Queen Ismena and everyone else.*

She hadn't spat it. She hadn't wept or thrown a tantrum. She had said it in that quiet, flat voice after a long pause—the voice of someone stating the weather, or the depth of a river she had already drowned in once—and then she had put her heels into her horse and ridden straight past the heir of the Autumn Court as if his orders were worth less than the mud on her boots.

Behind him, the four-horse coach crested the rise. Caelum took one look at Aurelie thirty yards up the trail ahead of the point-rider, then looked at Eris's rigid shoulders, and very wisely said nothing at all, though both of his russet fox ears flattened slightly against the rain.

*Just like you,* Eris repeated silently, his jaw set so hard a muscle ticked beneath the wet copper strand clinging to his cheek.

Three weeks ago, it had been the simple truth. When he had found a half-starved mortal girl in his woods with a living myth coiled around her like a guard hound, she had been nothing more than the leash attached to a weapon—a piece on the board that could tip Beron off his throne and keep Autumn from burning.

If she were still only the leash, he wouldn't have spent three nights listening to the silence outside her door at Ashgrove. He wouldn't have ordered Briar to pack six trunks of Autumn silk so Tarquin's court wouldn't see her in Summer colors. And he certainly wouldn't be sitting in the freezing drizzle with his blood hammering hot and sharp against his ribs because the stubborn little fool had just taken the point position on an unwarded mountain road where any border-stray or mercenary bowman would hit *her* chest before his.

He didn't spur the black gelding into a racing gallop that would spook her mare on the wet shale. Instead, he touched his heel once to the warhorse's flank and let the big stallion lengthen into a long, ground-eating canter that closed the thirty-yard gap in six silent, relentless strides.

Where the road curved sharply around an outcrop of wet black slate, an old stone pack-bridge arched over a white-water mountain gorge. Eris didn't call out. He simply brought the black gelding up on Brille's outside flank, matching the mare's speed smoothly, and leaned out of his saddle to catch the leather cheek-strap of Brille's bridle just above the bit—slowing both horses together in a controlled, curving arc until they came to a halt at the stone parapet of the bridge.

Only when all eight hooves were still did he release the mare's leather, though he left his black horse angled across Brille's chest so she couldn't bolt forward a second time.

"Try that again on an unwarded road," Eris said softly, his voice deadly calm over the roar of the white-water creek twenty feet below the stone arch, "and you won't like where you ride for the rest of the day, *Lady Delacroix*. Notice how I didn't say the carriage."

He swung down from the black gelding's saddle in one sharp, fluid motion, his boots hitting the wet flagstones with a solid thud.
